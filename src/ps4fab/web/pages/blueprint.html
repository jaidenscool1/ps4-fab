D · Diagnosis order for an unknown dead console

  1. Log it. Model sticker (CUH-xxxx), board revision once open, light pattern, beeps, fan behavior.
  2. Look. HDMI pins, burn marks, liquid residue, missing parts near the APU, swollen PSU caps.
  3. Swap the easy parts. Known-good HDD and PSU from one of the other consoles (same series only for the PSU).
  4. Measure before powering. Resistance to ground on the 12 V input and the APU rail. A near-zero reading means a short: find it before anything else.
  5. Clean and repaste. Fixes shutdowns and rules out heat.
  6. Back up the NOR with a SPI programmer before any board-level heat.
  7. Port and small-chip work first: HDMI port → HDMI encoder.
  8. BGA work last: southbridge reball → APU reball, on your least valuable board first. Preheat to ~150 °C, monitor with a thermocouple.

F · Bench procedures

Temperatures are starting points. Your thermocouple reading at the chip is what counts, not the number on the station.

F1
Repaste + fan cleanRemove top cover, PSU and heatsink. Clean old paste with 99% IPA, apply a pea-size dot to the APU die, clean the fan blades and heatsink fins. Do this on every console before anything else.
F2
NOR dump (#7)SPI programmer (CH341A with 3.3 V logic, or the 2.8 V mod) with a SOP16 clip, board unpowered, ~3.0 V only. Read it three times and compare the files. All three must match. Store them labelled with the console serial.
F3
HDMI port swap (#6)Kapton nearby plastics. Preheat board to ~100–120 °C. Add flux, heat the shell pins with hot air at ~380 °C until the port lifts. Clean pads with wick, tin, fit the new port, reflow, then check pins for bridges under magnification.
F4
APU / southbridge removalKapton and foil around the chip; remove anything plastic nearby. Preheat the board from below to ~150 °C. Top hot air with a large nozzle, circling, until the joint reaches ~230–240 °C (factory solder is lead-free, melts at 217 °C). Lift with a vacuum pen once the chip moves freely. Never force it, or you'll pull pads.
F5
Pad prepFlux, then drag wide wick with a flat iron tip until board and chip pads are flat and shiny. Clean with IPA. Check for lifted or missing pads before going on.
F6
Reball the chipThin flux on the chip, align the stencil (CXD90026G, 0.6 mm), pour 0.6 mm Sn63/Pb37 balls, brush the extras off. Heat gently until the balls go shiny and round (~200–215 °C, leaded melts at 183 °C). Let it cool, then lift the stencil.
F7
Refit the chipThin flux on board pads, align the chip to the silkscreen corners. Preheat to ~150 °C, top heat until the chip "settles" and self-centres (~210–220 °C at the joint). Do not press down. Cool slowly. Leave the bottom heater on and step it down.
F8
First power-onResistance check on the 12 V and APU rails first. Then a known-good PSU and HDD, heatsink fitted with fresh paste. Never power the APU without its heatsink, even for a second.

H · Firmware revert (fat), BwE method

Reverts only to the firmware in the backup slot (the one before the last update). NOR and syscon must be patched as a pair. Does not work on syscons marked "SIE INC".

Switching back and forth (Modded Warfare's "revertible firmware"): keep a matched NOR + syscon backup taken while the console was on each firmware, leave a Teensy 2++ wired inside, and restore the pair you want (~15 min per switch). Only works with your own console's backups. Each switch reinstalls system software, so the HDD is wiped. Two HDDs, one per firmware, avoid that.

  1. Dump NOR (#7) three times, compare (F2).
  2. Dump syscon (#8) with the Arduino glitch reader: SysconReader.exe COMx. On-board: lift pins 22 and 23 first. Dumps don't match → try 100/510/1k Ω resistors.
  3. Patch in BwE PS4 NOR Validator. NOR: Patch and Swap CoreOS, enable UART. Syscon: downgrade + service mode patches, enable debug.
  4. Program a blank syscon via FT232RL + Renesas Flash Programmer: export .bin to S28, 1-wire UART @ 115200, Erase/Program/Verify.
  5. Swap syscon: remove original, fit programmed blank. Write patched NOR. Keep the original chip and dumps.

I · Chain of trust (how a PS4 decides what to trust)

From power-on, each stage checks the next before handing over control. Standalone copy for sharing: PS4 Chain of Trust.

Same on every consolePer-console, locked to the APUFused in silicon

Simplified. The southbridge chip has two small processors of its own, EMC and EAP. The EMC loads its first loader from the NOR, and that loader starts the EAP kernel loader and the APU's boot ROM (MediaCon, Southbridge). The secure loader is stored in the NOR as sflash0s1.cryptx2 (Codenames). The wiki is community-written and marks parts of this as incomplete.

I1
Fused root keysBurned into the security processor at the factory. Used inside the chip only; never in the NOR, RAM or anywhere a probe reaches.
I2
Wrapped per-console keysStored encrypted in the NOR. Unlock this console's identity data and hard drive. Only this APU can unwrap them, so one console's NOR won't boot another board.
I3
Shared firmware keysSign and encrypt the system software; the same on every console and well studied publicly.
A jailbreak can
  • Run with full kernel control on that console
  • Ask its own security processor to unwrap its own keys
  • Make decrypted backups of its own NOR data and drive key
A jailbreak can't
  • Read the fused root keys from software (on the PS5 they reportedly leaked anyway, see the table)
  • Make one console's identity work on another board
  • Run on a console that doesn't boot, or on firmware newer than its bug

Questions to work through

  1. Where does each of your three consoles stop, judging by the symptom? Same place, or three different faults?
  2. How would you tell a hardware fault (power rail, cracked solder ball) from a firmware fault (bad NOR data, syscon mismatch)?
  3. If the root key is burned into the chip, why can't you measure it with a probe?
  4. Why does the hard drive have its own per-console encryption when the NOR is already protected?
  5. What does a repair look like at each stage: a replaced part, a rewritten chip, or nothing fixable?
